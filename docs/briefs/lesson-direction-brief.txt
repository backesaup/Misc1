Stance
This brief governs how every lesson is framed, not what it teaches. Apply it to every
lesson, script and question in the app, and re-read it before writing a new one.
The learner is a 5-year-old who is genuinely capable: he knows square and cube numbers,
loves arithmetic and asks real engineering questions. Treat him as a serious learner. He is
not a genius, and he is still only 5. Both halves matter.
Don't underestimate him. Use real ideas and real words (volts, parallel, perimeter,
remainder). Never baby talk, sing-song voice, fake excitement or "good job,
superstar!". Never dismiss a hard idea as "too grown-up".
Don't overestimate him either. Being bright doesn't mean he already knows the steps
in between. Being able to multiply doesn't mean he knows what area is. Assume
nothing he hasn't been taught.
Speak to him like a respectful, patient expert explaining something to a curious
person who just hasn't met this idea yet. Warm, calm, direct, plain words.
The goal is real learning: after a lesson he should be able to explain the idea back and
use it on something new, not just remember the ending.

The expert blind spot
The biggest risk in these lessons is you, the expert, skipping steps that feel obvious to
you. Psychologists call this the curse of knowledge: once you know something, it's very
hard to imagine not knowing it. In Elizabeth Newton's tapping study, people tapping out a
well-known song expected listeners to name about half of them; listeners got about 1 in
40. Education researchers (Nathan and Petrosino) call the teaching version the expert
blind spot: experts order and skip steps the way the subject is organised, not the way a
beginner learns it.
So before writing any lesson, do this:
1. Write the step chain. List every idea the learner must hold to reach the goal, from
what he already knows to the target. Example for "why a stack is 64": counting →
equal rows → multiplying as rows × columns → 8 × 8 → a cube as layers of squares → 4
× 4 × 4 → doubling → 64 reached by doubling.
2. Check every link. For each step, ask: does he already know this, or was it taught
earlier in this lesson? If neither, it's a missing step. Add it, or move the lesson later.

3. Hunt for hidden assumptions. Words he hasn't heard, symbols (×, =, −), units,
conventions ("rows go across"), and "obviously" moments. If you'd write "obviously",
"just", "simply" or "of course", there is probably a skipped step there.
4. Read it as him. Go through the script pretending to know only his starting knowledge.
Anywhere you'd have to guess, the lesson is broken.

First principles: understanding before procedure
Teach why something is true before how to do it. A rule he can rebuild himself is worth
more than ten he has memorised.
Start from something he can see or already knows, then build each new idea out of
the previous one. Never introduce a result he can't trace back to something earlier.
Show why it must be true, not just that it is. Not "area is length times width", but: lay
the blocks out in rows, count one row, notice every row is the same, so the total is
rows × blocks-per-row.
Let him reach the conclusion. Where possible, set up the pieces and ask him to
predict the next step before you state it. Being told the answer is weaker than nearly
getting there yourself.
Name the idea after he understands it ("this is called perimeter"), not before. The
word is a label for something he already gets.
Resolve contradictions immediately. If something seems to clash with what he
knows (54 × 60 mA is over 3,000, so why didn't the cube charge the phone?), make
that the centre of the lesson, not a footnote.
Connect ideas. Point out when a new idea is an old one in disguise (a cube is squares
stacked up; doubling 6 times lands on 64 again). Connections are what make
knowledge stick.
Concept, then procedure, then practice. Only after he understands should a lesson
give him a quick method to use.

Define everything
Nothing in a lesson may be undefined. Every word, number, symbol and unit must be
explained the first time it appears, in that lesson or an earlier one he has done.
Every number says what it counts. Not "60" but "60 milliamps, the amount of
electricity one panel makes". Not "9" but "9 panels on one face". When two numbers
are on screen, he must know which is which.
Every symbol is introduced before it's used. If × or = or a minus sign hasn't appeared
before, say what it means in words first, then show the symbol.

Every unit is grounded. A unit needs a picture or comparison he can hold onto
("milliamps measure how much electricity is flowing, like how much water comes out
of a tap"), and then the lesson must stay consistent with it.
One new term at a time. Never introduce two new words in the same step.
Keep a glossary. Each lesson lists the terms it defines and the terms it assumes. Any
assumed term must be defined in an earlier lesson. Claude Code should check this
automatically when building.
State assumptions out loud. "Let's pretend the sun is shining straight at it" is better
than quietly assuming perfect sunlight.
Use words consistently. If you've called it a "panel", don't switch to "cell" halfway
through without saying they're the same thing.

Testing understanding
Check understanding with questions he has to think through, never with "does that make
sense?" (a 5-year-old will almost always say yes).
Predict before reveal. Asking learners to commit to a prediction before seeing the
answer improves learning, even when the prediction is wrong (studied by Brod and
colleagues). Every lesson has at least one prediction, at the moment of the key idea.
Test understanding, not memory. A good question can only be answered by someone
who understood. Types that work:
What happens if…? ("If the cube had 4 panels on each face, how many
altogether?")
Which one is different, and why?
Use it on something new (the same idea in a different object).
Spot the mistake (a character gets it wrong; can he see where?).
Wrong choices must be real mistakes, not silly ones. Each wrong option should be
the answer a beginner would get from one specific misunderstanding (e.g. 8 + 8 = 16
instead of 8 × 8). Write down which misunderstanding each wrong option represents.
When he's wrong, find the broken step. Don't just show the right answer. Use the
misunderstanding behind his choice to go back to the step that failed, re-explain it a
different way, then let him try a similar question.
Mix in earlier ideas. Later lessons should include a question or two that need an idea
from a previous lesson, so knowledge is retrieved and connected, not left behind.
End by having him say the rule. The narrator asks him to say the rule out loud before
it's shown.

Stretching him and giving feedback
Aim each lesson one step past what he can do alone, close enough that he can reach it
with the lesson's help. This is Vygotsky's zone of proximal development: too easy teaches
nothing, too far just frustrates.
Always include a stretch. End lessons with an optional bonus question that goes a bit
further ("what's 4 × 4 × 4 × 4?"). Make it clearly a challenge, so not getting it isn't
failure.
Say when something is hard. "This part is tricky. Lots of grown-up engineers find it
tricky too" is honest and keeps him going. Pretending it's easy makes him feel bad
when it isn't.
Praise what he did, not what he is. Mueller and Dweck found that children praised for
being clever went on to avoid challenges and give up sooner after failure, while
children praised for their effort and approach took on harder problems. So: "you
noticed every row was the same, which is how you knew to multiply", never "you're so
smart".
Make feedback specific. Say exactly what was right or exactly which step went
wrong. No vague "nearly!" or "great job!".
Getting stuck is normal. Treat a wrong answer as useful information, calmly and
without fuss: "Let's see what happened."

Working with a 5-year-old's mind
Respecting him means designing for how a 5-year-old's mind actually works. The limits are
about capacity, not intelligence.
Small working memory. Young children can hold far fewer new things in mind at once
than adults. One new idea per step; keep the earlier pieces visible on screen so he
doesn't have to remember them.
Concrete before abstract. Real objects and blocks first, then pictures, then numbers
and symbols. Don't jump straight to "8 × 8"; show 8 rows of 8 blocks, then write it.
Short attention, so momentum matters. Keep steps short and let something happen
on screen every few seconds. A long talking pause loses him even if the content is
good.
Voice and pictures carry the lesson. Assume very little reading. Words on screen are
labels and numbers, and everything is also said aloud.
Numbers are his strength; use them. He loves arithmetic, so give him things to
calculate. A sum he can do is often the best way into a new idea.

Let him control the pace. He taps Next when ready and can replay any step. Never
rush him past a question.
Repetition with variation. Revisit a big idea in a new setting a few lessons later rather
than repeating the same example.

Creativity, engagement and stories
Every lesson should be genuinely creative and engaging, using everything the app can do.
A lesson that's just narration over a static picture is a missed opportunity.
Use every asset to the fullest
The voxel world: blocks fall, stack, rearrange, explode, grow and split apart. Show the
maths happening (8 rows sliding together into a square, a square folding into a cube)
rather than describing it.
3D and camera: fly inside things. Zoom into the cube's core, open up the iPad layer by
layer, follow electricity along a wire.
Interaction: he taps, drags, builds and breaks. He should do something every few
steps, not just watch.
Sound: satisfying sound effects for blocks landing, circuits connecting and correct
predictions, with a light music bed. Sound carries a lot of the energy.
Voice: use narration with character and timing (pauses, surprise, a dry joke), not a flat
read-out.
Variety: vary the format across lessons: a mission, a mystery, an experiment, a build
challenge, a "what went wrong?" investigation.
Teach through stories
Stories are easier to remember than lists of facts. The psychologist Daniel Willingham
calls them "psychologically privileged" because the mind follows causes, goals and
conflicts naturally.
Give the app a world and recurring original characters: for example an inventor who
builds things and a sidekick who asks the questions he might ask. Keep their
personalities consistent across lessons.
Frame lessons as problems to solve. The village needs a staircase up a cliff; the
inventor's phone is dead and only the solar cube can save it; a storage room only holds
64 blocks per slot, so why? The idea being taught must be what solves the problem.
Let characters make mistakes he can catch. The sidekick adds 8 + 8 instead of
multiplying, or points the cube's shaded side at the sun. Spotting their mistake is a
great test of his understanding.

Link lessons into a bigger story so finishing one leads into the next (the solar cube's
battery is charged, so now what can the inventor build?).
The story serves the learning. Keep the setup short, so the idea arrives within the
first 15–20 seconds. Cut any story beat that delays or distracts from the concept.

Balancing the method
The principles above are the default, not a rigid formula. These points refine them; where
they seem to conflict, these win.
Watch him, don't assume. This method suits a careful, older learner. A 5-year-old may
learn differently. Watch which lessons he replays, where he gets bored and what he
asks about, and adjust the approach to fit what actually works for him.
Make room for play. Young children learn a lot through free exploration. Include a
sandbox mode with no questions and no goals: a block world where he can build
squares, cubes and stacks, and a counter that shows how many blocks he's used.
Analogies are allowed, but make them honest. At his age, comparisons ("milliamps
are like how much water comes out of a tap") are often the only way into an abstract
idea. Use them, then point out where the analogy stops working as the topic goes
deeper.
Wonder can come first. Not every lesson has to open with a derivation. It's fine to
start with the surprising thing ("this cube can charge a phone!") and explain why
afterwards. Curiosity is what brings him back.
Let him struggle a little. After a wrong answer, give him one more try with a small hint
before re-explaining. Productive struggle helps learning; rescuing him too fast
doesn't.
The real test is him explaining it. The app can't hear him explain yet. Each lesson ends
with a "Talk about it" card for a grown-up: one or two questions to ask him ("why did
the cube not charge the phone much?"), plus what a good answer includes.
Hands-on beats screens. Each lesson ends with a real-world challenge where
possible: build a 3 × 3 × 3 cube from Lego, count the pieces, find 64 things in the
house, test which chair wobbles. Keep sessions short; the app is a starting point, not
the whole lesson.

Before any lesson ships, check:
Step chain written; every link is known or taught earlier.
Every number, unit, symbol and new word is defined at first use; one new term per
step.
The why is shown before the rule; the rule is named only after it's understood.
At least one prediction at the key moment.
Every wrong option maps to a named misunderstanding, with a re-explanation for it.
At least one question needs the idea applied to something new.
A stretch question at the end.
No baby talk, empty praise, "obviously", "just" or "simply".
Read through as him: no point where he'd have to guess what something means.
Any analogy used is flagged where it stops being accurate.
A "Talk about it" card for a grown-up and, where possible, a hands-on challenge.
He does something (tap, drag, build) every few steps; the idea is shown in motion, not
just described.
If there's a story, the problem in it can only be solved with the lesson's idea, and the
idea arrives within 20 seconds.
