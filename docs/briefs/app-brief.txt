Overview
Build an offline-capable web app (PWA) for iPhone and iPad that teaches a very bright 5year-old how real things are engineered, how to build machines in Minecraft, and new
maths, through short animated, narrated lessons where he guesses before each answer is
revealed.
How to use this brief (for Claude Code): read it fully before writing code. Work milestone
by milestone (see Milestones), keep commits small, and stop to ask Esa before any
decision that is hard to undo (framework swap, hosting, data collection). Where this brief
marks a fact as verify, do not ship it until Esa confirms it.
Goals for v1
1. A lesson player that runs any lesson defined as data, with animation, narration and
tap-to-guess questions.
2. One finished maths short: "Why is a stack 64?"
3. One finished engineering breakdown: the solar-powered Rubik's cube.
4. Installable to the home screen, works offline, no accounts, no ads.
Non-goals for v1: native App Store app, user accounts, analytics, AI chat, multiplayer.

The learner and design principles
The user is a 5-year-old who thinks well ahead of his age. He knows square and cube
numbers, loves arithmetic and is curious about how things are engineered, but knows
little other maths. He plays Minecraft (Bedrock edition, on iPad). Assume he reads very
little: everything important must work through voice and pictures.
Design principles
Voice first. Every step is narrated. Text on screen is short labels and numbers only.
Guess before reveal. Each lesson pauses at least once and asks him to commit to an
answer (tap one of 2–4 big choices) before showing it. Wrong answers get a calm
"let's see why", never a buzzer or a fail screen.
Real ideas, simple words. Don't water down the concept (volts vs milliamps, series vs
parallel, powers of 2). Simplify the words, not the idea.

Hard constraints
These apply to every lesson, scene and asset. Do not work around them.
Use Minecraft's Art Style. Use the blocky voxel style. Use Minecraft's textures,
sounds, fonts, logo, UI, or any of its characters or mobs, and recreate them closely.
Naming. The app's own name contains no Minecraft branding. Minecraft build
tutorials may say in narration that they are "for Minecraft (Bedrock)" as a plain fact.
Game facts must be accurate for Bedrock edition (e.g. stack size 64, cake has 7 bites,
the world goes down to Y = −64). Redstone behaves differently in Java and Bedrock;
build tutorials target Bedrock only.
Privacy. No accounts, no analytics, no third-party trackers, no network calls after
install except fetching app updates. Any progress (lessons completed) lives in
localStorage only, wrapped in try/catch.
No external links reachable from the child's screens.
Accessibility. Captions toggle in the parent screen; respect reduced-motion settings
with a simpler animation path.

First breakdown: the solar-powered Rubik's cube
Source: the YouTube video "I built a Solar-Powered Rubik's Cube" by Z3Cubing (June
2026), which he watched. A maker covered a 3×3 cube with 54 small square solar cells and
built it into a working, though impractical, phone charger. Details below come from a
second-hand summary; every number marked verify must be checked against the video
by Esa before the lesson ships.
He already knows a phone needs over 1,000 mA to charge and that one cell gives about 60
mA. The lesson builds on that.
1. What it does. A Rubik's cube covered in 54 tiny solar panels, 9 on each face, that can
charge a phone. Scene: the cube rotating in sunlight. Guess: "How many panels?" (he
can work out 9 × 6).
2. Light into electricity. Each panel turns sunlight into a little electricity: about 60 mA in
full sun (verify the per-cell voltage too; the summary said 1.5 V, which conflicts with
step 5).
3. Teamwork: series and parallel. The 9 panels on a face are joined in a chain (series),
which adds up the push (volts). The 6 faces are joined side by side (parallel), which
adds up the flow (milliamps). Scene: animated wiring, tap a face to see its chain light
up.
4. The battery. Power goes through a charge controller into small batteries in the cube's
hollow core. Key idea: energy can be saved now and used later.
5. The booster and USB port. A converter turns the battery's power into exactly 5 V for
the phone, through a USB port hidden under the white centre. (Verify: if each face's 9
cells in series gave 9 × 1.5 = 13.5 V, it wouldn't need boosting up to 5 V. Roughly 0.5 V
per cell, or 4.5 V per face, would fit a step-up, so the 1.5 V figure is probably wrong.)

6. The really hard part: it still turns. The pieces move, so how does electricity get
between them? Springy brass ball bearings press on brass contacts (96 of them),
magnets help pieces line up, and slip rings let power pass through the spinning
centres without wires twisting. Scene: cut-away of one edge piece touching its
neighbours. This is the best engineering bit; give it time.
7. Did it work? Guess first: "54 × 60 mA, how much is that?" (3,240 mA). Then the real
test: only 1–3 faces face the sun at once, there are gaps, and real output was at most a
couple of hundred mA, well short of the 1,000 mA a phone needs. It barely charged.
Final rule: engineers build it, test it, and find out the real number.
Scenes needed: voxel cube with cell texture and rotation; series/parallel wiring diagram
with current flow; battery fill gauge; cut-away of an edge piece with spring contacts; a
sun-angle view showing lit vs shaded faces.

Shape: hook question → pause to guess → reveal → one-line rule. Build the series in this
order; each starts from something he knows (squares, cubes, arithmetic) and takes one
step further.

Script: Short 1, "Why is a stack 64?" (about 45 s)
1. Scene: blocks drop into a slot and the counter climbs to 64, then stops. Voice: "A stack
always stops at 64. Not 50. Not 100. Why 64?"
2. Scene: 64 blocks rearrange into an 8 × 8 flat square. Voice: "You know this one. 8 rows
of 8." Guess: 16 / 64 / 88. Reveal: 64.
3. Scene: the square folds into a 4 × 4 × 4 cube. Voice: "And it's also a cube! 4 times 4
times 4." Guess: "Is that 64 too?" Yes / No. Reveal: yes.
4. Scene: one block, then it doubles: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, each step stacking up. Voice: "Start
with 1 and keep doubling. 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 …" Guess: "What comes next?" 48 / 64 / 40.
Reveal: 64.
5. Voice: "Computers love doubling, so builders pick numbers like 64. It's a square, a
cube and a doubling number, all at once."
6. Rule on screen and spoken: "64 = 8 × 8 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 1 doubled 6 times."
The "computers love doubling" line is a simplification; keep it, but don't claim that's
officially why the game uses 64.

Milestones, open questions and later
Work in this order. Each milestone ends with a deploy to Vercel and a check on a real
iPhone/iPad.
1. Setup. Vite + TypeScript project, PWA manifest and service worker, home screen with
three section tiles, deploy to Vercel. Done when it installs to an iPhone home screen
and opens offline.
2. Lesson player. Loads lesson JSON, plays narrated steps, question steps with tap-toguess and reveal, player controls, captions toggle. Use placeholder audio (Web
Speech) for now.
3. Voice pipeline. Kokoro bm_lewis generation script, hashing, smoke test that fails on
missing audio.
4. Short 1: "Why is a stack 64?" Voxel scenes (stack counter, 8 × 8 square, 4 × 4 × 4 cube,
doubling). Done when Esa watches it on the iPad with his brother.
5. Solar cube breakdown. After Esa verifies the marked facts.
6. Shorts 2–5, then the first Minecraft build (TNT dropper), then the real-world
breakdowns.
7. Polish. Parent screen, reduced-motion path, progress ticks on lesson cards, app icon.
Definition of done for any lesson: every step has audio, at least one guess, a closing rule,
no Minecraft assets, facts verified (or marked draft), and it plays start to finish on iPhone
Safari with no console errors.
