Overview
The game section is a fourth area of the app: a real game first, built on the same core
mechanics as any good game (a world to explore, problems to solve, abilities that grow),
where the maths and engineering are the mechanics. It is not lessons with a game skin.
Why: most educational apps are "chocolate-covered broccoli", quizzes wrapped in game
art. In Habgood and Ainsworth's Zombie Division study, children learned more when the
maths was the combat itself than when it was quizzes between levels, and chose to play
that version about seven times longer.
How this fits the other briefs
Build brief: same tech stack, PWA, offline use. The game lives alongside the three
lesson sections.
Lesson Direction Brief: still governs how ideas are framed: define everything, no
skipped steps, respectful voice, stretch him. In the game, "testing understanding"
happens through play instead of questions.
Links between sections: lessons and shorts can become optional "how it works"
videos inside the game, and solving something in the game can unlock the matching
lesson.
Goal for v1: one small, polished playable slice (see First playable slice), tested with him
before anything more is built.

Design pillars and core loop
Pillars (every feature must pass all five)
1. The idea is the mechanic. Each concept is something he does to make progress. Test
for every puzzle: can he get past it without understanding the idea? If yes, redesign it.
2. The world gives the feedback. No right/wrong screens. The fence has a gap and the
sheep escape; the tank overflows; the door stays shut. Failure is visible, harmless and
makes him want to try again.
3. Understanding unlocks power. Progression comes from new tools and abilities
earned by understanding (learn area → unlock the floor-planner; learn parallel →
unlock wires). No points, stars, streaks or badges.

4. Simple to control, deep to play. Tap, drag and place are enough. Depth comes from
the problems, not the controls.
5. Engaging, not compulsive. Natural stopping points, short sessions, nothing designed
to make him play longer than is good for him.
Core loop
1. Explore: walk the world, find something broken, locked or needed.
2. Meet the problem: a character needs something (a pen for sheep, water for crops).
3. Experiment: build, test, watch what happens. Predictions are made through play
("will these blocks be enough?").
4. Understand: when it works, the idea is named and the sidekick sums it up in one line.
5. Unlock: a new tool, area or ability that makes bigger problems possible.
The loop repeats at growing scale: one puzzle, then an area of puzzles, then the whole
world changing as he restores it.

World, story and characters
He is 5, so the story and the game stay very simple. No lore, no plot to follow, no cast to
remember. If an idea needs more than one sentence of story, cut the story.
Setting: one small farm. That's the whole world for the first slice.
Characters: just two.
The player: a builder he can name.
The sidekick: a friendly little robot who needs help fixing the farm. It's the only
character who talks. It gives each task in one sentence, cheers when something
works, and sometimes makes a simple mistake he can spot.
The "story" is one line per puzzle: "My bed broke, can you build me a new one?", "The
sheep keep running away!", "The plants are thirsty." Each task is obvious from what he can
see.
Rules
One goal at a time, with no side quests, branching choices, dialogue trees or anything
he has to keep track of. Hidden surprises are fine (see Emotion, fun and exploration).
The farm gets visibly nicer as he fixes things. That's the only progression story.
New places (the mine, the workshop and so on in the mechanics catalogue) are added
only if he's clearly ready, one at a time, each just as simple.

Emotion, fun and exploration
Clear and simple isn't enough: he has to want to be there. A 5-year-old engages through
feeling, silliness and freedom.
Emotion: he's helping a friend
The robot has an expressive face and big reactions: thrilled when its bed is built, upset
when the sheep run off, a silly victory dance when something works. He is fixing
things for someone he likes, not completing tasks.
The robot remembers what he's built ("I love my bed!"), waves when he comes back,
and is a bit dramatic in a funny way. It never guilt-trips him for leaving or not playing.
He can name the robot and choose its colour.
Silliness and chaos
Failure is funny, never sad: sheep bounce over gaps like springs, an overfilled tank
erupts like a fountain, a wobbly tower slowly topples.
Things to knock down and blow up. A safe boom zone where he can stack towers and
set off cartoon TNT (an original design, no Minecraft TNT). Cause and effect is huge
fun at his age, and it can later teach balance and structure (why do some towers
survive?).
Exploration with no goal
The farm is full of things that react to a tap: chickens squawk and flap, trees drop
apples, the pond splashes, a mole pops up and ducks away, the scarecrow spins.
Small hidden surprises to stumble on: a secret cave behind the waterfall, a golden
chicken, a hidden robot friend. Nothing to collect or track; finding them is the reward.
New tappable surprises appear as the farm is fixed, so it keeps changing.
Hooks and pacing (the good parts of short-form video)
Every puzzle opens with a hook in the first 3 seconds: a sheep sprints off-screen, the
robot's bed collapses with a crash.
Something new happens every few seconds: a reaction, a sound, a movement.
Every puzzle ends with a big emotional payoff: the fix works, the robot celebrates, the
farm visibly changes.
Retention: competing with YouTube
The game has to pull him back as strongly as YouTube does. It wins by being something
YouTube can't be: made for him, starring a friend who needs him, in a world that changes
because of what he did.

Instant start. Opening the app drops him straight back into the farm where he left off,
with the robot waving. No menus, no loading screens, no taps before the fun.
Something new every day. Each day one new thing appears on the farm (a new
animal, a strange egg, a mysterious crate, a new boom-zone toy). Missing a day loses
nothing; it's just waiting for him.
Cliffhanger endings. Every session ends with the payoff and then a teaser: the robot
hears a rumble behind the locked barn door, an egg starts to wobble. He leaves
wanting to know what happens.
Bite-size episodes. Each puzzle plays like a 2–5 minute episode with a hook, a build
and a big finish, the same rhythm as a video he'd choose, but he's doing it.
A world that's visibly his. The farm fills up with everything he's built and every animal
he's helped. He can show it off, and the parent screen can save a picture to share with
family.
Surprise everywhere. Tappable things react in varied, funny ways, so exploring
always feels fresh.
Growing friend. The robot slowly gains new abilities, outfits and dances as the farm is
fixed, so there's always something to look forward to.
Left out on purpose: slot-machine style random rewards, streaks that punish missed
days, guilt messages ("the robot is sad you left"), push notifications, autoplay and
sessions with no end. These create compulsion rather than enjoyment, and their pull is
strongest on young children whose self-control is still developing.

First playable slice: the farm
One area of the valley, about 15–25 minutes of play across several sessions, teaching area,
perimeter and volume. Each starts from squares and cubes, which he already knows.
Build only this slice first.
Key mechanic: the block store. Before building, he chooses how many blocks or pieces to
take from a store using a big number dial. That choice is his prediction. Taking too few
leaves a visible gap; too many leaves a visible pile. No one tells him he was wrong.
1. The sidekick's bed (area). The robot's bed broke. A glowing 3 × 4 outline on the
ground. He places blocks one by one. After the first row, the sidekick notices every
row is the same length. A second outline (5 × 3) appears with no counting help: he
dials how many blocks to take first. Unlock: the floor tool (drag to fill a rectangle in
one go), earned because he now knows how many it will use.
2. The sheep pen (perimeter). The sheep keep escaping. Fence pieces go along the
edges of a grass patch. A 4 × 4 patch needs 16 pieces; any gap and a sheep wanders
out. Then the twist: there are only 16 fence pieces and wants room for as many sheep

as possible. He tries shapes: 4 × 4 holds 16 sheep, 2 × 6 holds 12, 1 × 7 holds only 7.
Same fence, different space inside. This is the moment area and perimeter separate in
his head. Unlock: the fence tool.
3. The water tank (volume). The crops need water. The tank is a 3 × 3 × 3 box; each
bucket fills one block. He sees it fill layer by layer: one layer is 9 (3 × 3), three layers
are 27 (3 × 3 × 3). He dials his buckets before pouring, and the tank overflows or stops
short. Optional next step: a 4 × 4 × 4 tank where the pump fills a whole 16-block layer
at a time, so 4 pumps make 64, which links to the stack-of-64 short. Unlock: the water
pump.
4. The watchtower (stretch). Rebuild a 3 × 3 × 3 tower that is hollow inside. The store
has exactly 26 blocks. Why not 27? Finding the missing middle block is the payoff. It's
also the number of pieces on a Rubik's cube, a hook into the solar cube story.
Every puzzle must have: a one-line setup from a character; the idea needed to succeed;
visible, funny, harmless failure; a sidekick line naming the idea after success; and a hint if
he's stuck for about 60 seconds (a nudge, never the answer).
Slice is done when: it plays smoothly on iPad and iPhone, all four puzzles are completable,
and he can explain one of the ideas in his own words afterwards.

The solar workshop is the climax: it uses the real ideas from the solar Rubik's cube
breakdown, so the lesson and the game reinforce each other.

Simple, obvious and guided
As a game, it must be super simple and obvious. He should never wonder what to do next
or how to do it. Depth lives in the ideas, never in the controls or the menus. Where this
conflicts with anything else in this brief, simplicity wins.
Always one clear goal. A single glowing marker shows where to go and what to do.
Never more than one active task at a time.
Show, don't explain, the controls. The first time any action is needed, a hand icon
demonstrates it (tap here, drag this) and the sidekick says it aloud. Repeat the demo
if he hesitates.
Hand-hold the doing, not the thinking. The game guides him firmly on how to act
(where to tap, what to drag) but leaves the idea for him to work out, with hints that
get stronger if he's stuck.
Hint ladder: about 20 s stuck → the sidekick restates the goal; 40 s → it points at the
key thing ("look, every row is the same"); 60 s → it does the first step with him. It never
gives the final answer outright.
One new thing at a time. One new control or one new idea per puzzle, never both
together.

Few choices on screen. Only the blocks and tools needed for the current puzzle; no
inventory management, crafting trees or menus to navigate.
No reading needed. Every instruction is spoken and shown with icons.
Nothing can get stuck or lost. He can't fall off the world, break a puzzle or lose
progress. Undo and "reset this puzzle" are always available.
Keep puzzles small. Few blocks per puzzle (under about 30), and twists like the
sheep-pen shapes or the hollow tower are separate, optional steps after the basic
version.

Controls, feel and healthy engagement
Controls
Tap the ground to walk there; tap an object to interact. No virtual joystick.
Drag a block from the hotbar to place it; tap a placed block to pick it back up. Undo is
always one tap away.
Camera: one finger rotates around the build, pinch to zoom; it auto-frames the current
puzzle so he never gets lost.
Number dial for choosing quantities: big plus and minus buttons, number shown large
and spoken aloud.
Every interactive thing is at least 64 pt and clearly highlighted.
Game feel (this is what makes it feel like a real game)
Blocks snap, bounce and click into place; water sloshes; sheep wander and react.
Short, satisfying celebration when a machine works: the windmill starts turning, lights
come on. The world changing is the reward.
Characters react with personality, and small hidden surprises reward exploring.
Music shifts as areas come back to life.
Healthy engagement
Each puzzle is a natural stopping point; the game saves automatically.
No timers, lives, energy bars, daily rewards or streaks.
A gentle, optional session limit set from the parent screen; when reached, the
sidekick goes to sleep and the game ends with a cliffhanger, not a hard cut.
No dark patterns: nothing that makes stopping feel like losing something.

Tech, milestones and playtesting
Tech notes

Same stack as the build brief: Vite + TypeScript + Three.js, PWA, offline, Vercel.
A simple grid-based voxel engine: blocks on integer coordinates, one unit each. No
physics engine; outcomes (gap in fence, tank overflow) come from rule checks on the
grid, then animate.
Puzzles are data files (goal, allowed blocks, success check, hint ladder lines, unlock),
so new puzzles don't need new engine code.
Save progress in localStorage (wrapped in try/catch); keep frame rate smooth on an
older iPad by limiting draw distance and merging block meshes.
Narration through the same Kokoro bm_lewis pipeline as the lessons.
Milestones
1. Grey-box: walk, place and remove blocks, camera, number dial, one puzzle (the bed)
with plain shapes. Test the controls with him straight away.
2. The farm slice: all four puzzles, the sidekick, hints, unlocks, narration.
3. Polish: game feel, sound, music, celebrations, the valley visibly changing.
4. Playtest, then decide what to build next from the mechanics catalogue.
Playtesting with him (the most important step)
Watch him play without helping. Note where he hesitates, taps the wrong thing or
gets bored; those are design bugs, not his mistakes.
Afterwards, ask him to explain one idea ("why did the fence need 16?"). If he can't, the
mechanic didn't teach it.
Best sign it's working: he asks to play again on his own.
Open questions
Keep the valley and workshop setting, or something else he'd love more?
Names for the robot sidekick and the valley (let him choose?).
Main device: his iPad, your iPhone, or both?
